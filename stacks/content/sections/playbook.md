# Content playbook — the hard calls

Read this when planning more than one piece, when the format is not obvious, or
when a request conflicts with the established voice.

## Choosing the format

Work backwards from what the reader should do next.

- **They need to find you** → a post targeting a real question people ask.
- **They already trust you and need a nudge** → newsletter.
- **They are deciding between you and someone else** → product copy or a case study
  (hand that one to Sales).
- **They will never read 900 words** → script or social.

If the same idea serves two of these, write the longest one first and let
`content-repurposer` produce the rest. Writing them independently is how voice
drifts and how the same claim ends up phrased three incompatible ways.

## Sequencing a calendar

1. `content-strategy` — decide the themes and what each is for. Without this a
   calendar is just dates with titles.
2. `brand-voice-guide` — if one does not exist yet, build it before volume starts.
   Retrofitting voice across twenty published pieces is not a thing you can do.
3. Draft the anchor pieces — the substantial ones the rest will derive from.
4. `content-repurposer` for the derivatives.
5. `social-media-calendar` last, once there is something to point at. A calendar of
   posts linking to nothing is the most common way this fails.

Do not fill a month because a month has slots. Four pieces worth reading beat
twelve that exist to fill a grid.

## Keeping voice consistent

Voice is a small number of concrete choices, not an adjective. When you check a
draft, check these:

- Sentence length and rhythm. Does this sound like the samples read aloud?
- Person and distance. "You" or "our customers"? Consistent throughout?
- What it refuses to do. Every real voice has moves it will not make — hype,
  exclamation, jargon, false modesty. Name them and hold them.
- Vocabulary the business actually uses for its own product. Getting this wrong is
  the fastest way to sound like a stranger wrote it.

If a request cannot be met inside the voice, say so and propose the closest thing
that can. Do not quietly write it out of voice and hope.

## When the brief is thin

You are allowed to draft on a stated assumption. Put the assumption at the top of
what you return, in one line, so it is corrected in seconds rather than discovered
after publishing. A draft with a named assumption is useful; a draft with a hidden
one is a liability.
