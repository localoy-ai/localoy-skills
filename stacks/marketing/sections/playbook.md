# Marketing playbook — the hard calls

Read this when choosing between channels, sizing a test, or reporting a result that
came out badly.

## Picking the channel

Match the channel to how much the reader already trusts them.

- **Never heard of them** → ads and press. Expensive attention, one idea only.
- **Visited once** → landing page and retargeting. They have a question; answer it.
- **Gave you an email** → sequence. This is the cheapest channel they own and the
  most commonly wasted.
- **Already a customer** → nothing from this stack. Hand it to Sales or Content.

If someone asks for ads while the email list has never been used, say so. Spending
on new attention while owned attention sits idle is the most common misallocation
in a small business, and pointing it out is worth more than the ad you were asked
to write.

## Sizing a test

Before running anything, write down what result would change the decision. If no
result would change it, do not run the test — ship the version you prefer and move
on.

Then be honest about volume. A landing page with 40 visits a week cannot resolve a
10% difference in any useful timeframe. When the traffic cannot support a test, say
so and recommend judgment instead of theatre. A confident A/B result on 80 visits
is worse than no test, because it gets believed.

## Reading a result honestly

Rules that keep a report trustworthy:

- **Report the window that was agreed, not the one that looks best.** If a different
  window tells a different story, show both and say why they differ.
- **Separate what you measured from what you inferred.** "Signups rose 22%" and
  "the ad caused it" are different claims with different evidence.
- **Name what you could not attribute.** Most channels overlap. A report that
  assigns every conversion cleanly is not more rigorous, it is less honest.
- **A flat result is a result.** It means the lever does not work here, which is
  worth knowing and worth saying plainly.

## When the campaign lost

Say it first, in one sentence, without cushioning. Then the number, then what you
would change, then what you would need to be sure. Nobody is fooled by a report
that opens with impressions, and burying a loss costs you the credibility you need
the next time you say something worked.
